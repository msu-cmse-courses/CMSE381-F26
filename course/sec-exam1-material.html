    Supervised learning
    • Outcome measurement \(Y\) (also called dependent variable, response, target, label).
      🔗
      🔗
    • Vector of \(p\) predictor measurements \(X\) (also called inputs, regressors, covariates, features, independent variables).

    • In the regression problem, \(Y\) is quantitative (e.g price, blood pressure).

    • No outcome variable, just a set of predictors (features) measured on a set of samples.

    Section Stats Review

    This is intended to be only a brief review of some concepts from stats. If you need a more thorough refresher, please use your stats book or other online resources.
    🔗

    Sampling distribution.

    The objective of statistical inference is to draw conclusions about the population using a sample from that population.
    🔗
    A statistic is any quantity whose value can be calculated from sample data. Before the data is obtained, we are uncertain about the value that the statistic will assume. Therefore, a statistic is a random variable. In particular, a sample mean \(\bar{X}\) has a probability distribution.
    🔗
    🔗

    Hypothesis testing.

    Suppose we want to know if exposure to asbestos is associated with lung disease.
    🔗
    Procedure:
    🔗
    • Take some rats and randomly divide them into two groups:
      • Group 1: expose to asbestos
        🔗
      • Group 2: leave unexposed
        🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    • Compare the disease rate in the two groups. Consider the following two hypotheses:
      \begin{equation*} H_0: \text{The disease rate is the same in the two groups} \end{equation*}
      \begin{equation*} H_1: \text{The disease rate is not the same in the two groups} \end{equation*}
      🔗
    • Only use hypothesis testing to test well-defined hypotheses.
      🔗
      🔗
    • In hypothesis testing, we assume \(H_0\) is true unless there is strong evidence to reject \(H_0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • We assume that the samples are drawn at random from two independent normal populations.
      🔗
    • A hypothesis of the form \(\theta = \theta_0\) is called a simple hypothesis.
      🔗
    • A test of the form \(H_0: \theta = \theta_0\) versus \(H_1: \theta \ne \theta_0\) is called a two-sided test.
      🔗
    • The most common tests are two-sided.
      🔗
    Suppose that we have two treatments with samples taken for each treatment such that:
    🔗
    1. The experiment was performed using a completely randomized design. This means that:
      1. The population was randomly sampled.
        🔗
      2. The data are normally distributed.
        🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    2. The two treatments come from two populations with identical variance.
      🔗
    Depending on whether we know the variance or not, we will use the \(Z\)-test and the \(t\)-test, respectively. For our class, we will be working only with the \(t\)-test.
    🔗
    🔗

    \(t\)-test.

    In practice, we often do not know the variance. In that case, the test statistic is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} t_0 = \frac{\overline{y}_1-\overline{y}_2} {S_p\sqrt{\frac{1}{n_1}+\frac{1}{n_2}}}, \end{equation*}
    where \(S_p\sqrt{\frac{1}{n_1}+\frac{1}{n_2}}\) is called the standard error of the difference in the means, abbreviated as \(se(\overline{y}_1-\overline{y}_2)\text{,}\) and
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} S_p^2 = \frac{(n_1-1)S_1^2+(n_2-1)S_2^2} {n_1+n_2-2}. \end{equation*}
    To determine whether to reject \(H_0: \mu_1=\mu_2\text{,}\) compare \(t_0\) to the \(t\)-distribution with \(n_1+n_2-2\) degrees of freedom (DOF):
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \text{If } \lvert t_0\rvert > t_{\alpha/2,\,n_1+n_2-2}, \text{ reject } H_0. \end{equation*}
    This procedure is called the two-sample \(t\)-test.
    🔗
    Example 31. Portland cement formulation experiment.
    Tension bond strength data is given fo the Portland cement formulation experiment are given below. (Source: Design and Analysis of Experiments, D.C. Montgomery, 8th edition.)
    🔗
    Formulate the null hypothesis that the two formulations yield the same tension bond strength. Then, compute the corresponding \(t\)-statistic.
    🔗
    Choosing \(\alpha=0.05\text{,}\) we would reject \(H_0\) if \(t_0 > t_{0.025,18}=2.101\text{,}\) or if \(t_0 < -t_{0.025,18}=-2.101\text{.}\) Do we reject \(H_0\) in this case? What is the corresponding conclusion?
    🔗
    Solution.
    The test hypotheses are
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} H_0: \mu_1=\mu_2 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} H_1: \mu_1\ne\mu_2. \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} S_p^2 = \frac{(n_1-1)S_1^2+(n_2-1)S_2^2} {n_1+n_2-2} =0.081, \end{equation*}
    so \(S_p=0.284\text{.}\) The test statistic is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} t_0 = \frac{\overline{y}_1-\overline{y}_2} {S_p\sqrt{\frac{1}{n_1}+\frac{1}{n_2}}} =-2.20. \end{equation*}
    Because \(t_0=-2.20 < -t_{0.025,18}=-2.101\text{,}\) reject \(H_0\) and conclude that the mean tension bond strengths of the two formulations are different.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Confidence intervals.

    We are often interested in knowing how much the means differ instead of only whether they differ. The interval within which the parameters would be expected to lie is called a confidence interval.
    🔗
    To obtain an interval estimate of \(\theta\text{,}\) we need to find two statistics \(L\) and \(U\) such that the probability statement
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} P(L \leq \theta \leq U) = 1-\alpha \end{equation*}
    is true. The interval \(L \leq \theta \leq U\) is called a \(100(1-\alpha)\) percent confidence interval for the parameter \(\theta\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Interpretation of the confidence interval: A \(95\%\) confidence level implies that \(95\%\) of all samples would give an interval that includes whatever parameter is being estimated. Specifically, if we take a large number of samples, and for each one construct a confidence interval (CI) of the form \(L \leq \theta \leq U\text{,}\) then in the long run, with \(95\%\) confidence for example, the constructed interval will contain the parameter \(\theta\) \(95\%\) of the time.
    🔗
    Therefore, the confidence interval is not so much a statement about any specific realization of the CI; instead, it pertains to what would happen if a very large number of like intervals were constructed using the same formula.
    🔗
    🔗

    Section Assessing Coefficient Estimate Accuracy

    Bias in estimation.

    • Assume a true value \(\mu^*\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • An estimate from training data is \(\widehat{\mu}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • The estimate is unbiased if \(E(\widehat{\mu})=\mu^*\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • The sample mean is unbiased for the population mean:
      \begin{equation*} E(\widehat{\mu}) = E\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_i X_i\right) = \mu. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    • The standard variance estimate is biased
      Note 38.
      (underestimation)
      🔗
      🔗
      :
      \begin{equation*} E(\widehat{\sigma}^2) = E\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_i (X_i-\overline{X})^2\right] \neq \sigma^2. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Linear regression is unbiased.

    • 100 data points drawn from \(Y=2+3X+\epsilon\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\epsilon\) is drawn from a normal distribution with mean \(0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • The red line is the true relationship; the blue line is the least-squares estimate.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Repeat this 10 times and plot all the resulting lines in variations of blue.
      🔗
      🔗
    • The resulting models are slightly different, but all lie around the true relationship shown in red.

    Variance in estimation.

    • True value \(\mu^*\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Estimate from training data \(\widehat{\mu}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Variance of the sample mean:
      \begin{equation*} \operatorname{Var}(\widehat{\mu}) = \operatorname{SE}(\widehat{\mu})^2 = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    • Standard error measures by how much a sample statistic, e.g, \(\hat{\mu}\) differs from the true population value, e.g., \(\mu\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • The more data you have, the smaller the variance and the better the estimate.
      🔗
      🔗
    Note 41. Standard error versus standard variation.
    Standard deviation measures the amount of spread in a sample from the mean. Standard error measures the statistical accuracy of a sample statistic (like a mean), i.e., (how much it deviates from the true population mean).
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Variance of linear regression estimates.

    • Variance of linear regression estimates: \(\operatorname{SE}(\widehat{\beta}_0)^2 = \sigma^2\left[ \frac{1}{n} + \frac{\overline{x}^2} {\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\overline{x})^2} \right]\) \(\operatorname{SE}(\widehat{\beta}_1)^2 = \frac{\sigma^2} {\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\overline{x})^2},\) where \(\sigma^2=\operatorname{Var}(\epsilon)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      Aside
      If data are more spread out, the standard error is smaller.
      🔗
      🔗
    • The residual standard error is an estimate of \(\sigma\text{:}\) \(RSE=\sqrt{RSS/(n-2)}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Confidence Interval.

    The \(95\%\) confidence interval for \(\beta_1\) approximately takes the form \(\widehat{\beta}_1 \mathbin{\pm} 2\cdot\operatorname{SE}(\widehat{\beta}_1).\)
    Note 42.
    The same form works for \(\beta_0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Interpretation: There is approximately a \(95\%\) chance that the interval \(\left[ \widehat{\beta}_1-2\cdot\operatorname{SE}(\widehat{\beta}_1), \widehat{\beta}_1+2\cdot\operatorname{SE}(\widehat{\beta}_1) \right]\) will contain \(\beta_1\) when we repeatedly approximate \(\widehat{\beta}_1\) using repeated samples.
    🔗

    Hypothesis testing.

    • \(H_0:\) There is no relationship between \(X\) and \(Y\text{.}\)
      Aside
      Null hypothesis.
      🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(H_1:\) There is some relationship between \(X\) and \(Y\text{.}\)
      Aside

    Test statistic and \(p\)-value.

    Test statistic:
    \begin{equation*} t=\frac{\widehat{\beta}_1-0} {\operatorname{SE}(\widehat{\beta}_1)}. \end{equation*}
    \(t\)-distribution with \(n-2\) degrees of freedom.
    🔗
    Note 45.
    A small \(p\)-value indicates that it is unlikely to observe such a substantial association between the predictor and response by chance, in the absence of a real association between them.

    Assessing the accuracy of the module: Residual Standard Error (RSE).

    RSE
    \begin{equation*} RSE = \sqrt{\frac{RSS}{n-2}} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n-2}\sum_i (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    • Estimate of the standard deviation of \(\epsilon\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Average amount that the response will deviate from the true regression line.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Assessing the accuracy of the module: $R^2$.

    R squared:
    \begin{equation*} R^2 = \frac{TSS-RSS}{TSS} = 1-\frac{RSS}{TSS} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    where total sum of squares is
    \begin{equation*} TSS = \sum_i (y_i - \overline{y})^2 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    • TSS is total variance in the response \(Y\text{,}\) variability before regression.
      🔗
      🔗
    • RSS is the amount of variability after the regression.
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(R^2\) is the proportion of variability in \(Y\) that can be explained using \(X\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Close to 1: a large proportion of variability is explained by the regression.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Close to 0: the regression does not explain much of the variability in the response.
      🔗
      🔗
